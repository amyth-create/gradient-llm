Finding a separation method in 25 injections: random search, Bayesian optimisation and an LLM chemist on three simulated mixtures

Three synthetic analyte sets stand in for unknown mixtures. A deterministic simulator turns any linear-gradient method into a retention table and a chromatographic response function (CRF) score, pre-computed on a 22 080-method grid per set. Three optimisers query that table one injection at a time with a budget of 25. This report shows what the samples and tables contain, how the CRF landscape looks, how each search walks through it, and how often each of the three reaches the optimum over 50 repeated campaigns per set.

1. The three simulated samples

Each sample is a list of hidden analytes. Reverse-phase retention follows the linear-solvent-strength model with a van 't Hoff temperature term: for analyte i, log10 k = log kw − S·φ + ΔH (1/Tref − 1/T). Three numbers per analyte therefore define a sample: log kw (retention in pure water at 30 °C), S (how fast retention falls with organic fraction) and ΔH (how fast it falls with temperature). The optimiser never sees these; it only sees the retention table a method produces.

Difficulty is set by how many analytes are near-twins. Two analytes with close log kw and close S co-elute over most of the method space and can only be pulled apart by a narrow choice of slope and temperature. The easy set has no such pair, the medium set two, the hard set three.

2. How the data was prepared

Everything a search sees comes out of one deterministic pipeline: a seeded generator draws the hidden analytes, a simulator turns any method into a retention table and a score, the simulator is evaluated on a round-number grid to make the tables, and a dense grid plus local refinement finds the reference optimum each search is scored against. No measurement noise is added anywhere; two runs of the same method give the same row.

Step 1 · Generating the samples

Analytes are drawn by rejection sampling from uniform ranges, and a draw is kept only if it differs from every accepted analyte by more than 0.3 in log kw or more than 1.0 in S. Difficulty is then imposed by overwriting rows into near pairs, and a set is accepted only if its reference optimum reaches a CRF floor. The sets used here are seeds 0, 1000 and 2000 of that procedure.

Step 2 · Simulating a method

Retention. Isocratic retention is the linear-solvent-strength relation with a van 't Hoff temperature term, log10 k = log kw + ΔH (1/Tref − 1/T) − S φ, with T in kelvin and Tref = 30 °C. S is temperature-independent. Two analytes whose log k lines cross swap elution order at φ = Δlog kw / ΔS, which is why the near pairs respond to the φ window and not only to slope.

Gradient elution. The program is linear from φ0 to φ1 over tG, then held at φ1. It reaches the column inlet 0.20 min late (dwell) and the outlet a further 0.45 min later (dead time). The analyte's migration integral is solved in closed form in three regimes: eluted before the gradient front arrives, eluted during the gradient (the textbook LSS case, with steepness b = t0 S Δφ / tG), or still on the column when the gradient ends and finishing in the hold at φ1. The closed form is checked against a Runge-Kutta integration of the same model in the test suite.

Peak width. σ = √[(t0 (1 + ke) G / √N)² + σec²] with N = 15 000 plates, ke the retention factor at the moment of elution, G = 1 − 0.25 b / (1 + b) the gradient band-compression factor for a peak eluting inside the gradient (G = 1 otherwise) and σec = 0.02 min of extra-column dispersion, so no peak is narrower than that.

Truncation. Acquisition stops at 42 min, which is the 45 min maximum run minus 3 min re-equilibration. Peaks after that are dropped from the table and counted as missing. The spec made such runs infeasible; the implementation keeps them feasible with a poor score instead, because an operator does see a truncated chromatogram, and a cliff from −1.6 to "no data" at 42.0 min would have cut an otherwise smooth landscape.

Score. CRF = [Σ over adjacent observed pairs of min(R / 1.5, 1)] / (n − 1) − 0.5 · max(0, (tlast − 10) / 10), with R = ΔtR / (2 (σi + σi+1)). A missing analyte is one pair with zero credit and sets tlast to 42 min, so the floor is −1.6. The maximum of 1.0 needs every pair at R ≥ 1.5 and the last peak within 10 min: the best method is the shortest fully resolved one. Pressure was dropped from the study because the 600 bar limit never binds on this grid (worst case about 530 bar).

Step 3 · The table and the reference optimum

The table is the simulator evaluated on every in-bounds point of a round-number grid (φ in steps of 0.05, tG in steps of 2 min, T in steps of 5 °C), which is what the next section lists column by column. The reference optimum is found off-grid: a 30 × 30 × 30 × 8 grid over (φ0, Δφ, tG, T), 216 000 points evaluated in batch, then Nelder-Mead from the best point. A search is scored against that CRF, and "found" means within 1 % of it. On all three sets the optimum is 1.0, and the best grid row also reaches 1.0, so the discrete table loses nothing at the top.

3. What is in the simulated table

For each set, tables/<set>.csv holds one row per method on a round-number grid inside the instrument bounds: φ0 0.05 to 0.60 and φ1 0.15 to 0.95 in steps of 0.05 (with φ1 ≥ φ0 + 0.10), tG 2 to 40 min in steps of 2, and T 25 to 60 °C in steps of 5. That is 12 × 17 × 20 × 8 minus the φ1 constraint, or 22 080 methods per set. The row is everything the instrument would report for that injection and nothing about the analytes.

The instrument is a 100 × 2.1 mm column with 1.8 µm particles, N = 15 000 plates, 0.5 mL/min, dead time 0.45 min, dwell 0.20 min. Acquisition stops at 42 min (45 min maximum minus 3 min re-equilibration); analytes still on the column then are not observed, the row stays feasible with a short table and a poor score, and n_missing counts them. No row of the default grid is infeasible.

5. How each search navigates the landscape

All three optimisers share one contract: a TableOracle over the set's table, a budget of 25, one row per query, a row never charged twice, regret measured against the stored optimum. Random search draws a seeded permutation of the rows. Bayesian optimisation (BO) uses the same first eight random rows, then fits a Gaussian process (ARD Matérn-5/2, hyperparameters refit each step) and picks the unqueried row with the highest expected improvement. The LLM search starts from a blank page: an Azure OpenAI deployment (gpt-6-astra) reads the retention table of every injection so far and proposes the next method as JSON, which the harness snaps to the grid.

6. Results: fifty campaigns per method

Each method was run 50 times per set with 25 injections per campaign. For random search and BO the campaign number is the seed of the row permutation; for the LLM search it only labels the repeat, since the model's proposals are nondeterministic and every campaign starts from a blank page. 7. The LLM search

Architecture

The Python harness owns the budget, the grid, the logging and the conversation. The model answers one question per injection: given everything so far, which method next? Nothing from the analyte parameters, the optimum or any unqueried table row crosses the dashed boundary.

System prompt

The text below is optimizers/llm_system_prompt.txt verbatim, the fixed first message of every conversation. It gives the instrument, the grid, the scoring rule and standard linear-solvent-strength method-development practice. It is generic across sets: it does not state the number of analytes (the first user message does) and carries no hint about where optima tend to lie.

You are developing a reverse-phase HPLC gradient method for a mixture whose
composition you do not know. You choose the method; the instrument runs it and
returns the chromatographic result. Your goal is the highest CRF score reachable
within a fixed number of injections.

## Instrument (fixed for the campaign)
- Column 100 x 2.1 mm, 1.8 um particles, N = 15 000 plates. Flow fixed at 0.5 mL/min.
- Dead time t0 = 0.45 min. Dwell (gradient delay) tD = 0.20 min: the programmed
  gradient reaches the column inlet at 0.20 min and the outlet 0.45 min after that,
  so at the outlet the gradient runs from 0.65 min to tG + 0.65 min.
- Extra-column dispersion sigma_ec = 0.02 min; no peak is narrower than that.
- Data acquisition stops at 42 min (45 min maximum run minus 3 min
  re-equilibration). Compounds that have not eluted by then are not observed:
  the retention table is shorter and you are told how many are missing.

## Method (what you choose)
A linear gradient from phi0 to phi1 (organic fraction) over tG minutes, then a hold
at phi1 until the run ends, at column temperature T.
The instrument only runs grid values; anything else is rounded to the nearest one:
- phi0: 0.05 to 0.60 in steps of 0.05
- phi1: 0.15 to 0.95 in steps of 0.05, and phi1 >= phi0 + 0.10
- tG:   2 to 40 min in steps of 2
- T:    25 to 60 C in steps of 5

## What a run returns
The retention table: one row per observed peak sorted by retention time, with
t_R (min), sigma (peak standard deviation, min) and the resolution to the next
row, R = (t_R,next - t_R) / (2 (sigma + sigma_next)); plus the CRF and its two
terms. The mixture has a known number of compounds (stated in the first
message). When all of them elute within 42 min the table has exactly that many
rows: co-elution shows up as R near 0, not as a missing row. Rows carry no
identities. The same compound can be row 3 in one run and row 5 in another when
the elution order changes, so compare runs by pattern, not by row number.

## The score
CRF = [sum over adjacent observed pairs of min(R / 1.5, 1)] / (n - 1)
      - 0.5 * max(0, (t_last - 10) / 10)
with n the number of compounds in the mixture.
- A pair earns full credit at R >= 1.5 and nothing beyond it, so the score is lost
  at the worst pairs.
- A last peak after 10 min costs 0.05 per extra minute.
- A compound missing at 42 min counts as one pair with zero credit, and t_last is
  then taken as 42 min, so a truncated run pays 1.6 on the time term alone.
  The floor is -1.6.
- 1.0 is the maximum: every pair at R >= 1.5 with the last peak within 10 min.
  The best method is therefore the shortest fully resolved one.

## Retention model (what the chemistry lets you infer)
Reverse-phase retention follows the linear-solvent-strength model: for each
compound log10 k = log kw - S * phi at a given temperature, with compound-specific
log kw (roughly 1.5 to 4.5) and S (roughly 3 to 10). Raising temperature lowers
retention for every compound by a compound-specific amount, so T shifts
selectivity modestly (it can reorder a close pair) and shortens the run.
Consequences you can use:
- The organic fraction at which a peak leaves the column is about
  phi_e = phi0 + (phi1 - phi0) * (t_R - 0.65) / tG, clamped to [phi0, phi1].
  A peak at or before about 0.6 min is essentially unretained: lower phi0.
  A peak after tG + 0.65 min eluted during the hold at phi1: raise phi1 or lengthen tG.
- Trimming the gradient window to the elution window (phi0 a little below phi_e of
  the first peak, phi1 a little above phi_e of the last) keeps every peak inside
  the gradient and spends the run time where the peaks are.
- Gradient steepness b = t0 * S * (phi1 - phi0) / tG sets resolution. Shallower
  (longer tG, narrower window) spreads pairs whose S differ and widens peaks a
  little; steeper compresses and shortens. A pair whose members have nearly equal
  S barely responds to slope and needs T or a different phi window instead.
- Two runs that differ only in tG show which pairs respond to slope (their R
  changes a lot) and which do not.

## How to work
- Propose exactly one method per injection. Each injection spends one unit of
  budget; a proposal that rounds to a method already run is wasted, so stay on the
  grid and do not repeat.
- Early injections buy information: where the elution window is, which pairs are
  critical, how they respond to slope and temperature. Later injections exploit it.
  Keep the best method in mind and refine around it as the budget runs out.
- Once every pair is at R >= 1.5, the remaining gain is time: shorten tG, narrow the
  window or raise T until a pair starts to fall below 1.5, then back off one step.
- Avoid truncated runs: a shallow gradient ending at a low phi1 can hold the most
  retained compound on the column past 42 min.

Reading the result




In the seed-0 campaigns shown in section 5 the LLM search found a CRF 1.0 method on every set within five injections and spent the rest of the budget looking for faster or more robust methods at the same score; section 6 shows how that holds up over 50 campaigns. Two things make the behaviour plausible rather than lucky. The prompt tells the model that the best method is the shortest fully resolved one and how slope, window and temperature act on resolution, so its first scouting gradient already lands in the dark region of the landscape, and each later move is one grid step with a stated reason. And the hard set's difficulty is that the good region is small, not that it is hidden: a reader who knows the chemistry can walk to it.


With 50 campaigns per method the comparison is symmetric in budget and repeats; it is not symmetric in information, since the LLM search carries a domain prior in its prompt that neither baseline has. The follow-ups that would measure that prior are a warm-started variant sharing BO's first eight injections, an ablation without the retention-model section of the prompt, and a memorisation check on a freshly generated analyte set.
